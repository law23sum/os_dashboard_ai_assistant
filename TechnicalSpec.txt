0.1 Mission, Product Scope & Strategic Objectives
0.1.1 Mission
The OS Dashboard AI Assistant (“OS Dashboard”, “the OS”) is a driver-aware AI operating system for knowledge and
execution work.
Its mission is to:
  • Govern, automate, and augment the full lifecycle of high-value work across:
       ○ Documents, code, research, simulations, writing, finance, security, and operations.
  • Turn real workflows into executable Capsules (repeatable workflows + environments) that can be run, audited, shared,
      and improved over time.
  • Provide a single, auditable control surface over:
       ○ OS and Unix execution, package & environment topologies,
       ○ SaaS and infra APIs, databases and research tools,
       ○ Policies, billing, compliance, and regulator views.
  • Enable governed autonomy: AI agents can act on files, tools, systems, and infra—but only through governed drivers
      and Capsules, under explicit policy and with full traceability.
The system must serve simultaneously as:
  • A local polymath workstation (desktop + CLI) that behaves like a personal “Jarvis” on a single machine.
  • An enterprise AI OS (multi-tenant backend + governance + marketplace) that can be adopted by teams, companies,
      and regulators, with regulator-grade audit and policy enforcement.
  • A foundation for cross-org / civilization-scale coordination through higher Meta-Stack layers (Advanced → Super →
      Hyper → Ultra → God).

0.2 Design Principles, Constraints, Assumptions & Non-Goals
0.2.1 Core Design Principles
 1. Auditable by construction
     Every meaningful action (model call, driver call, Capsule run, environment change, policy decision) is recorded in the
     Project Ledger and observable via Record Auditor and Evidence Packs. The system must always be able to answer:
     “Who/what did what, when, under which policy, with which environment and inputs?”
 2. Driver-first, not tool-first
    All external capabilities (OS, Unix, package managers, SaaS APIs, DBs, lab tools) are exposed as drivers with
    manifests, not opaque tools or ad-hoc shell access. Unix/Kernel execution and package managers are treated as
    first-class, governed drivers, not side channels.
 3. Plane separation (Data / Control / Governance)
    Data motion (CIR, Ledger, indices), control decisions (plans, driver orchestration), and governance decisions (policies,
    budgets, compliance) are logically separated, even when co-located physically. This enables reasoning, scaling, and
    safety.
 4. Local ≡ Enterprise (conceptually)
    Local and Enterprise modes share the same conceptual architecture and domain model. Differences lie in scale, driver
    scope, trust boundaries, and governance strictness—not in vocabulary or semantics.
 5. Domain-neutral substrate, domain-rich surfaces
    Core abstractions (Projects, Tasks, CIR, Capsules, Drivers, Policies, Twins) are domain-agnostic. Workspaces and
    Engines compose these into domain-specific UX and behavior (Dev, Research, Writer, Cyber, Finance, etc.).
 6. Governable automation
    Automation is always subject to policy evaluation, budget checks, and safety harnesses—especially for System
    Execution and Package & Environment drivers. There is no unguarded “root shell” for agents.
 7. Self-improving over time
    The platform continuously learns from execution traces, Capsule performance, Ledger history, and TRF/AIC insights to
    refine:
      ○ Plans and strategies,
      ○ Environment specs and blueprints,
      ○ Capsule templates and packs,
      ○ Policies and guardrails.
 8. Layered Meta-Stack with graceful degradation
    Advanced/Super/Hyper/Ultra/God layer capabilities are explicit and optional. Each higher layer must degrade cleanly if
    disabled, without corrupting the core.
 9. Local-first optionality & offline resilience
    Local Mode must be able to function with degraded or zero network connectivity and, where possible, with local or
    cached models, deferring sync of ledger and evidence.
10. Explainable cognitive behavior
    Personas and daemons must produce reasoning traces, references, and justifications suitable for human review, audit,
        ○ Capsule templates and packs,
        ○ Policies and guardrails.

0.2.2 Constraints & Assumptions
  • Runs on top of existing OSes
     OS Dashboard is not a bare-metal kernel; it runs atop Windows, macOS, and Linux.
  • Unreliable external systems
     All external systems (APIs, CLIs, OS environments, SaaS) are assumed unreliable; the design must include retries,
     backoffs, fallbacks, idempotency, and degraded modes.
  • Heterogeneous estate
     Must support mixed environments:
       ○ Windows/macOS/Linux, on-prem and cloud,
       ○ Modern SaaS, legacy UIs, air-gapped lab machines.
  • Multi-model, evolving Model Layer
     Multiple model providers and on-prem models will coexist, with evolving capabilities; the Model Layer is pluggable and
     cost-aware.
  • Regulated contexts
     Enterprise Mode assumes regulator presence or future regulator interest; explainability, reproducibility, and data
     residency are non-negotiable requirements in many deployments.
  • Sandboxed execution
     For risky workflows (system commands, package installs, simulations), containerization/virtualization and Safety
     Harnesses must be available.
0.2.3 Non-Goals (for this spec / v1)
  • Replacing every existing MDM/endpoint or SIEM system; instead, integrate with and augment them.
  • Prescribing a single vendor for models, storage, or orchestration; these remain pluggable.
  • Providing full UX design or visual styling specifications beyond what is needed to express architectural requirements
     (personas, approvals, evidence views, etc.).
  • Implementing a generic programming language or low-level VM beyond what Capsule and Policy representations
     require.
  • Serving as a general-purpose, consumer-grade chat assistant unrelated to projects, drivers, or Capsules.

       ○ External organizations or OS Dashboard instances participating via HyperMesh and Inter-OS Knowledge Network,
           with identity and trust contracts.

0.6.2 Daemon Runtime & Governance
  • Scope
       ○ Per-project, per-workspace, per-tenant, or Meta-Stack-wide.
  • Triggering
       ○ Event-driven (ledger events), scheduled, or user-initiated.
  • Budgets
       ○ Token, CPU, driver call, and time limits.
  • Policy Envelope
       ○ Permitted actions and required escalation paths for high-risk operations.
All daemon actions:
  • Emit ledger events.
  • Are observable via Record Auditor.
  • Must provide human-readable summaries when they change user-visible state.

0.7.2 Core Interaction Pattern
 1. Intent capture
         ○ User or persona expresses a goal via a Workspace (e.g., “deploy this service”, “run this simulation”, “prepare
             audit pack”).
 2. Planning & reasoning
         ○ Personas (Sora, Aria, AIC variants) and daemons generate a plan:
               § Which Capsules and drivers to use,
               § Which environments or twins to target,
               § What preconditions and approvals are needed.
 3. Governance evaluation
         ○ Policy Engine evaluates plan:
               § Identity, roles, data classification, budgets, risk thresholds.
         ○ May require human approvals or regulator-defined checks.
 4. Execution
         ○ Orchestrator runs Capsules and drivers according to the approved plan.
         ○ System Execution and Package drivers are invoked as governed, logged operations.
 5. Observation & learning
         ○ Outcomes recorded in Ledger, CIR, metrics.
         ○ Daemons mine traces to update:
               § Capsule templates, environment specs, plans, and policies.
 6. Packaging & sharing
         ○ High-value workflows are generalized into Capsule Packs, My Stack Capsules, and Marketplace entries.
Critically, models never directly call OS or external resources; they only propose plans that are realized via drivers and
Capsules under policy control.

       ○ Certain tenants, workspaces, or data classes may be restricted to:
             § Local or on-prem models only.
       ○ Model outputs that drive high-risk actions (system commands, environment changes, finance transactions) must
           pass through:
             § Safety harnesses,
             § Policy evaluation,
             § Potential human review.
  • Failure Modes
       ○ Provider outage:
             § Fallback to alternate provider or local model where allowed.
             § Gracefully degrade feature set; suspend risky autonomous flows.
       ○ Provider API change:
             § Handled at the provider abstraction layer.
       ○ Unexpected behavior/drift:
             § Detected via Critic Daemons, Alignment Monitor, Canon of Truth checks.

1. Architectural Overview & Principles
This section defines the end-to-end architecture of the OS Dashboard AI Assistant as a layered, plane-aware, driver-first AI
operating system. It unifies the “frozen architecture doc” with the driver fabric, Unix/System Execution Layer, and Package
& Environment Drivers, and anchors the role of the Driver-Aware Orchestrator in the overall stack.
It answers:
  • What are the core layers and components?
  • How do intents flow from users/agents to drivers and back?
  • What principles constrain design and evolution?
  • How does this map to Local / Enterprise / Hybrid deployments?
  • What is considered the Core Platform envelope before Meta-Stack capabilities?
1.1 Logical Layered Architecture
The platform is organized into six logical layers:
 1. Presentation Layer
 2. Application Layer (assistant_core Orchestrator & services)
 3. Cognitive / Reasoning Layer (personas, daemons, TRF)
 4. Domain / Model Layer (projects, tasks, CIR, capsules, policies)
 5. Infrastructure Layer (storage, queues, search, compute)
 6. Security, Governance & Billing Layer
These are conceptual boundaries: in Local Mode they may co-reside in a single process; in Enterprise Mode they are split
across services.

2. Planes Architecture: Data, Control & Governance
This section formalizes the three primary planes of the OS Dashboard AI Assistant—Data Plane, Control Plane, and
Governance & Policy Plane—and defines how they interlock across Local, Enterprise, and Hybrid deployments.
Each plane is a conceptual slice of responsibility, not necessarily a separate deployment tier. The same physical component
may participate in multiple planes, but with different roles and guarantees in each.
All higher-order behavior (workspaces, driver fabric, capsules, Meta-Stack, HyperMesh) is built as compositions over these
planes.
2.1 Data Plane – Definition, Responsibilities & Boundaries
The Data Plane is the system’s memory and record of reality. It contains:
  • The facts of what exists (projects, documents, code, configurations, environments, twins, capsules, policies).
  • The history of what has happened (ledger events, logs, metrics, traces, policy decisions).
  • The interpreted structure of those facts (CIR, indices, graphs, archives).
It does not decide what should happen (that is the Control Plane), nor whether something is allowed or economically
acceptable (Governance & Policy Plane). It simply records, serves, and indexes.
2.1.1 Purpose & Responsibilities
The Data Plane is responsible for:
  • Persisting core content and state:
        ○ Projects, tasks, workspaces, Master Stack entries.
        ○ CIR Documents & Blocks (normalized representations of documents, code, notes, logs, research artifacts).
        ○ Capsule manifests, Capsule Packs, Blueprints, My Stack Capsules.
        ○ Digital Twin and Cognitive Twin definitions and state snapshots.
        ○ Policies, Policy Packs, Safety Harnesses, Compliance Packs (as data objects).
  • Persisting temporal & causal records:
        ○ Project Ledger events (append-only, tamper-evident).
        ○ Policy evaluations and governance decisions.
        ○ AI usage and billing events.
        ○ Environment changes (package installs/upgrades/removals, blueprint convergence, drift detections).
  • Persisting observability & telemetry:
        ○ Metrics (time-series).
        ○ Structured logs.
        ○ Traces (cross-component execution paths).
  • Providing query surfaces & indices:
        ○ Full-text search.
        ○ Semantic/vector search.
        ○ Knowledge and dependency graphs.
        ○ Time-series & analytical queries over metrics and events.
  • Supporting time-travel, replay & reconstruction:
        ○ Reconstruct project state and environment “as of” a point in time.
        ○ Replay workflows and investigate incidents.
        ○ Provide inputs for research backtesting and Meta-Stack analysis.
Key invariant: All durable state that matters to OS Dashboard must be representable inside the Data Plane and reachable
via structured queries.

In scope:
  • Core content & configuration

        ○ Project and workspace metadata and settings.
        ○ Capsule manifests, tests, environment specs, execution traces.
        ○ Twin definitions, parameters, and state histories.
        ○ Policy & governance definitions and versions.

2.4 Cross-Plane Flows & Invariants (How Planes Interlock)
Behavior is constrained by cross-plane invariants that connect Data, Control, and Governance.
 1. Every side-effectful action is governed and logged.
       ○ Any operation that:
             § Modifies CIR, code, configs, envs, or twins.
             § Executes commands via System Execution Layer.
             § Calls external services or writes to external systems.
       ○ Must:
             § Be represented as a Plan/Workflow step in Control Plane.
             § Be evaluated by Policy Engine (or fall under explicit allowlist).
             § Emit Ledger events (and often telemetry) into Data Plane.
 2. No direct writes bypass Control Plane.
       ○ Data Plane writes come only from:
             § Control Plane actions that were governed.
       ○ Direct DB/file writes by arbitrary components are prohibited (or strictly sandboxed and logged as exceptional).
 3. Planning must be Data-aware and Governance-aware.
       ○ Plan generation (Cognitive Layer + Orchestrator) uses:
             § Data Plane for state and history.
             § Governance for constraints and budget envelopes.
       ○ Personas/AIC should avoid proposing plans that are impossible or illegal under the current policy/budget
           environment.
 4. Governance decisions are themselves data.
       ○ Every allow/deny/approval/transform decision:
             § Becomes a Ledger event.
             § Can be inspected by Record Auditor, Regulator, and Meta-Stack layers.
       ○ Policies are versioned and stored in Data Plane, enabling:
             § “Why was this allowed then but denied now?” style introspection.
 5. Observability spans all three planes.
       ○ Control Plane:
             § Emits metrics/logs/traces.
       ○ Governance Plane:
             § Emits metrics/logs/traces for policy evaluations and approvals.
       ○ Data Plane:
             § Stores observability and ties it to ledger events and entities.
       ○ Meta-Stack:
             § Uses the combined signal for systemic analysis.
 6. Least privilege and explicit scope.
       ○ Control Plane components operate within:
             § Explicit driver scopes, policy domains, and data sets.
       ○ Data Plane access is:
             § Scoped and filtered by Governance, even for internal daemons.

2.5 Failure Domains, Isolation Boundaries & Failure Containment Across Planes
Plane separation defines failure domains and containment strategies.
2.5.1 Data Plane Failures
Examples:
  • DB outage or corruption.
  • Object store unavailability.
  • Search/vector index lag or failure.
  • Metrics/logs backend overload.
Effects:
  • Control Plane may:
       ○ Lose ability to fetch complete context or write events.
  • Governance Plane may:
       ○ Temporarily lose access to recent usage and policy evaluation logs.
Containment & Strategies:
  • Enter read-only or degraded mode:
       ○ Block destructive or high-risk operations if ledger and audit integrity cannot be guaranteed.
       ○ Allow limited low-risk actions using cached context (configurable).
  • Signal:
       ○ Health issues to operators via metrics and alerts.
  • Prevent:
       ○ Silent data corruption by treating inconsistent storage as a blocking condition for sensitive operations.
2.5.2 Control Plane Failures
Examples:
  • Orchestrator crash.
  • Workflow engine overload.
  • Daemon scheduler failure.
  • Driver worker pool exhaustion.
Effects:
  • New plans may not be generated.
  • In-flight workflows may stall or be slowed.
  • Data Plane and Governance remain intact.
Containment & Strategies:
  • Use idempotent PlanSteps and ledger-based checkpoints:
       ○ On restart, reconstruct state from Data Plane and resume where safe.
  • Apply backpressure and prioritization:
       ○ Throttle non-critical workflows.
       ○ Prioritize incident remediation and critical business flows.
  • Governance may:
       ○ Temporarily restrict initiation of high-risk or expensive plans until Control Plane stabilizes.
2.5.3 Governance & Policy Plane Failures / Misconfiguration
Examples:
  • Policy Engine unavailable or degraded.
  • Conflicting policies causing deadlocks or global deny.
  • Misapplied Compliance Pack.
Effects:
  • Control Plane cannot safely determine allow/deny for new actions.
  • Risk of under- or over-restricted behavior.
Containment & Strategies:
  • Default behavior:
       ○ Fail-closed for high-risk operations (system execution, package/env changes, data exports, production twin
          mutations).
       ○ Fail-open or cached-policy for low-risk operations (e.g., read-only, purely local, or tenant-configured exceptions),
          with explicit flags and warnings.
  • Emergency “break-glass” policies:
       ○ Allow designated roles to:
            § Override or bypass normal policy evaluation for specific actions,
            § With mandatory justification, heavy logging, and automatic Evidence Packs.
  • Safe rollout of policy changes:

    ○ Fail-open or cached-policy for low-risk operations (e.g., read-only, purely local, or tenant-configured exceptions),

Tenant Isolation

       ○ Per-tenant DBs, schemas, or strong row-level isolation.
  • Control Plane:
       ○ Tenant-aware queues, worker pools, and quotas.
  • Governance:
       ○ Per-tenant policy sets and compliance packs.
       ○ Explicit cross-tenant flows governed and logged.
Execution Isolation
  • System Execution and Package & Env drivers:
       ○ Prefer execution in containers/sandboxes/bastion hosts.
  • Capsules:
       ○ Namespaced per tenant/project.
       ○ Denied cross-tenant effects unless explicitly federated with strict policies.
  • Meta-Stack operations:
       ○ Scoped explicitly to allowed sets of tenants/projects; cross-tenant operations require explicit Multi-OS/HyperMesh
           governance.

4. Cognitive Agents, Reasoning & Daemon Framework
This section defines the cognitive layer of OS Dashboard AI Assistant: personas, AIC, daemons, the Theoretical Reasoning
Framework (TRF), the per-project “brain”, and the way all of these interact with Capsules, drivers, policies, and the three
planes (Data, Control, Governance).
Cognitive entities are strategy surfaces over the shared substrate defined in Sections 2 and 3. They:
  • Read from the Domain & Knowledge Model (Users, Projects, Tasks, CIR, Ledger, Capsules, Policies, Twins).
  • Propose plans, analyses, and transformations.

  • Emit events, reasoning traces, and evidence into the Data Plane.
They never bypass drivers, Capsules, or policies.

4.9 Cognitive Safety, Alignment, Guardrails & Escalation Patterns
4.9.1 Guardrail Principles

             § Compares actual actions to declared goals, policies, and canon.

       ○ Persona warns: “High-risk; please confirm,” and surfaces reasoning summary and alternatives.

       ○ Parameters: business inputs (IDs, dates, amounts).
       ○ Outputs: files, screenshots, parsed screen content.
Safety & Failure
  • Unexpected UI changes or prompts:
       ○ Capsule halts, collects context (screenshots), raises task/incident.
  • Malfunctioning flows:
       ○ Put capsule into “needs maintenance” state and notify Operator workspace.
  • All credentials managed via secrets & policies; no secrets in capsule manifests.

  • Python: pip, conda, pipx, poetry.
